# CS430 — Course Examination EX01: What If the Other Service Never Answers?

**Course:** Distributed Systems and Reliable Workflows · **Coverage:** CS430-L01–L05 and practical interview extensions  
**Audience:** Full-stack/backend engineer responsible for real business outcomes  

This is not a distributed-systems theory oral defense. Imagine you are the engineer asked to keep a booking, payment, or background workflow reliable. Reason from what the system **can know**, what it **cannot know**, and how to proceed safely.

# Part I — Questions

## Round A — When calls become uncertain

### Q01 — The provider charged the customer, maybe
Your backend calls a payment provider and times out. The customer clicks Pay again. What can you infer from the timeout, and what architecture prevents an accidental double charge?

### Q02 — The retry fix caused an outage
A dependency slows down. Your service retries three times, the dependency gets even slower, and the entire fleet begins failing. What could be happening? What would you change first?

### Q03 — The system has ten-second timeouts everywhere
A user-facing request has a 12-second deadline but calls four downstream services, each with a ten-second timeout. Why can this be an incoherent design? How would you manage time budgets?

### Q04 — The service is sick but not dead
A third-party API intermittently fails and begins consuming all your Node.js outbound connections. How do you prevent one dependency from exhausting the service? What must your fallback preserve?

## Round B — Different nodes, different realities

### Q05 — The booking vanished after failover
A customer receives success, the database primary crashes, and the booking isn't visible on the new primary. Describe plausible explanations and the product-level decisions needed to avoid misleading promises.

### Q06 — The user doesn't see what they just saved
A profile update returns success, but the next GET occasionally shows the previous value. The database uses read replicas. What could explain this and what user-visible guarantees would you offer?

### Q07 — The autoscaling experiment backfires
A backend scales from 4 to 30 instances under load. CPU on each instance falls, but database latency and HTTP errors increase. Why might more application servers make the system worse?

### Q08 — The queue grows while CPU looks fine
A system accepts 200 jobs per second and completes 150 per second. Worker CPU is moderate. What happens over time, and what information would you need before adding workers?

## Round C — Workflows crossing boundaries

### Q09 — Split this monolith into services?
Management wants separate Booking, Payments, Inventory, Email, and Reporting services because "microservices scale better." How would you decide whether to split, and where would you draw boundaries?

### Q10 — The missing event
A service commits a booking and publishes an event for analytics. Sometimes the record exists but no event arrives. Design a mechanism that makes recovery possible, including what the consumer must tolerate.

### Q11 — A newer status gets overwritten
Two events about the same order are delivered out of order. The dashboard regresses from `shipped` to `processing`. What would you change in the event and projection design?

### Q12 — Two services disagree about completion
The customer has paid, but the hotel provider rejected the reservation. There is no shared transaction across the services. How should the system represent and repair the situation?

### Q13 — The webhook arrives late and twice
A provider sends the same payment-success webhook twice, including after the customer cancelled. What should your webhook handler do—and what should it not assume?

### Q14 — A realistic architecture exercise
Design a booking workflow that continues safely when a worker crashes halfway through, a payment response is lost, and the browser closes. Explain what state must survive and how the system makes progress without depending on one process staying alive.

# Part II — Model Answers and Reasoning

### A01 — The provider charged the customer, maybe
A timeout establishes that the caller didn't receive a usable result within its deadline; it doesn't establish whether the provider acted. Persist a payment-intent identity and send a stable idempotency key that the provider recognizes where supported. On an ambiguous outcome, query or reconcile the provider's status before repeating an unsafe side effect. Keep a durable state machine: created, pending, authorized/captured, failed, uncertain. Handle simultaneous retries with uniqueness/atomic state transitions. If the provider lacks an idempotency facility, acknowledge the limits and use reconciliation, manual intervention, or carefully designed application-side deduplication. Never equate HTTP failure with business rollback.

### A02 — The retry fix caused an outage
Retries multiply demand on a dependency already struggling, potentially creating a positive feedback loop. Three retries across layers can mean far more than three actual attempts. Introduce bounded retries for transient errors only, exponential backoff with jitter, a per-request deadline, and a retry budget. Apply backpressure or load shedding at the entrance, and ensure circuit breakers or bulkheads prevent widespread exhaustion. Measure original requests, retry attempts, dependency success/latency, and queueing. Consider whether a stale or degraded response is appropriate for that operation. Reliability mechanisms must reduce load during failure, not automatically generate more work.

### A03 — The system has ten-second timeouts everywhere
Sequential downstream calls can collectively exceed the user's overall 12-second deadline, and retries magnify that. Propagate an overall deadline, budget time for each step, cancel unnecessary work when possible, and reserve time for an error/fallback response. A timeout must be chosen for the expected latency distribution and business importance, not copied from a convention. Parallelize genuinely independent calls while bounding concurrency. Cancellation is not rollback: a remote operation may have completed even after the client stops waiting. Track end-to-end latency, timeout locations, and outcomes rather than treating all timeouts as identical.

### A04 — The service is sick but not dead
Use bounded outbound concurrency and connection pools, per-dependency timeouts, backpressure, and isolation so one slow provider cannot consume every resource. A circuit breaker may temporarily stop attempts after enough failures and probe recovery cautiously. It doesn't make the provider healthy and shouldn't substitute for retries or business state handling. Fallback depends on the contract: a catalogue recommendation may use stale data; a payment charge cannot be fabricated. Show users a trustworthy pending/unknown state and reconcile later when needed. Measure pool waiting and failure concentration as well as raw CPU.

### A05 — The booking vanished after failover
The success acknowledgment may have come before the write was durably copied to a node eligible for failover, or the application may be reading a lagging replica. Examine replication mode, commit acknowledgment semantics, leader election/failover, and whether the old primary was fenced to prevent split-brain. The product must define what "confirmed" promises. Stronger acknowledgment can add latency or reduce availability during partitions, but losing an acknowledged reservation may be unacceptable. Also distinguish stale reads from actual data loss. Test failovers against explicit business invariants, not only whether a new primary becomes writable.

### A06 — The user doesn't see what they just saved
Asynchronous replicas may lag after a successful write. A read routed to a replica can temporarily return old data. If the product requires read-your-writes, route subsequent reads for the relevant session/entity to the primary, use a consistency token/replication position to wait for sufficient progress, or briefly pin the user to a fresh data path. Don't promise universal immediate consistency if the architecture doesn't provide it. State which displays tolerate eventual updates and which do not. Instrument replica lag and recognize that caches can create an additional stale layer even when the database is fresh.

### A07 — The autoscaling experiment backfires
Each new instance can open a pool of database connections and generate more concurrent work than the database can handle. For instance, thirty instances with twenty potential DB connections each creates up to six hundred competing connections. Lock contention, query I/O, pool wait times, and retry storms may dominate. Application CPU dropping simply means work is waiting somewhere else. Inspect end-to-end latency decomposition, DB connection counts and waits, slow queries, lock contention, and admission rate. Cap per-service concurrency, tune pools, introduce backpressure, and scale or optimize the actual constrained resource. Horizontal scaling moves bottlenecks; it doesn't abolish them.

### A08 — The queue grows while CPU looks fine
At a sustained 200 arrivals and 150 completions per second, backlog grows by fifty jobs per second: three thousand after a minute, 180,000 after an hour. Delay grows even if consumers aren't CPU-bound. Inspect processing-time distributions, external dependencies, database contention, worker concurrency, retries, and throughput versus useful completions. Add workers only if the downstream system can absorb them. Otherwise reduce ingress, prioritize jobs, coalesce duplicate work, reject lower-value tasks, or increase the real bottleneck's capacity. Queue depth without oldest-job age and completion throughput can hide worsening user experience.

### A09 — Split this monolith into services?
First identify independently evolving domains, team ownership, data ownership, scaling differences, and operational capability. Booking and inventory may share tight correctness constraints; splitting them casually can turn a local transaction into a distributed workflow. Reporting and email are often easier candidates for asynchronous boundaries. A modular monolith with explicit module contracts may be the safer starting point. Microservices are not merely directories with separate deployments: they add network failure, versioning, observability, security, and consistency costs. Extract when these costs buy meaningful autonomy or scale. A shared database written by several supposed services usually defeats strong ownership boundaries.

### A10 — The missing event
Database commit plus message publication is a dual-write problem. Put an outbox record in the same database transaction as the booking. A publisher reads pending records, sends them, and advances its durable progress; it can retry after crashes. Consumers must be idempotent because the same event may be delivered twice, especially if publishing succeeds but acknowledging publication fails. Keep event IDs and schema versions, and monitor outbox backlog. If data can be reconstructed by change-data capture, evaluate it against the same recovery requirement. Avoid telling management that "exactly once" is guaranteed merely because a broker offers a special delivery setting.

### A11 — A newer status gets overwritten
Event arrival order is not necessarily business-event order across partitions, retries, or consumers. Include a monotonic version/sequence per entity or another authoritative ordering contract, and update the projection only when the incoming event is newer than its current version. Make transitions explicit and idempotent. Don't assume timestamps from independent machines are perfectly ordered. Determine whether the dashboard is a derived eventually consistent view and what reconciles it with the source of truth. If ordering is essential, partition or serialize work by business key where feasible, but still guard against replayed old events.

### A12 — Two services disagree about completion
Represent the workflow as a persistent multi-step business process, not a single Boolean. Payment success and reservation failure require reconciliation or compensation: void an authorization, initiate a refund after capture, or attempt an alternative booking if business rules allow. Compensation is another fallible action, not time travel; it must be tracked, retried, and surfaced to operations. Decide which service coordinates progress (orchestration) and which owns each fact. Keep the customer informed with an accurate pending/refunding/failed state. Avoid distributed transactions that pretend remote provider actions can be atomically undone by rolling back your database.

### A13 — The webhook arrives late and twice
Authenticate the webhook according to provider instructions, persist receipt safely, deduplicate deliveries by provider event identity, and associate it with the correct payment operation. Then evaluate whether the event is compatible with the **current** workflow state. A late success after cancellation is evidence that the provider may have acted, not permission to reopen a cancelled booking automatically. Trigger reconciliation or compensation as required. Respond promptly and process durable work asynchronously where appropriate. Event deduplication is not enough: two distinct events can still describe the same business outcome. Your state machine and invariants remain authoritative.

### A14 — A realistic architecture exercise
Persist a booking intent with a stable ID, state transitions, inventory claim/hold, and external operation IDs. Use idempotent supplier/payment calls where possible, plus reconciliation for uncertain outcomes. A background worker claims durable pending work, performs one controlled step, records its result, and can be safely restarted or retried. Use conditional state transitions/leases carefully so two workers don't both advance incompatible actions. An outbox handles reliable notifications and downstream projections; an inbox/deduplication record handles webhooks. The browser only observes status and can reconnect at any time. Compensations and expiry paths are explicit. The goal is durable progress with truthful outcomes, not a guarantee that no individual action ever repeats.

## Closing perspective

The discipline of distributed systems begins when **"I didn't receive an answer"** stops being confused with **"nothing happened."** Reliable workflow design stores the uncertainty, controls repetition, and makes it resolvable.
